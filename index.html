The purpose

Dental school teaches you to care for patients. What about choosing how you’ll practice?

After years of coursework, clinic requirements, and preparing for licensure, the first job search can feel like another major exam. Many dental students are deciding between an established organization with business support and an independent office that may offer a different kind of professional experience. This guide is here to make those choices easier to understand, not to choose a path for you.

Reason 1: Make career decisions with context

A job offer is more than a starting salary. Scheduling, mentorship, clinical autonomy, contract provisions, benefits, patient mix, and opportunities for growth all matter. Comparing these details before accepting a position can help you avoid making an important decision based on only one attractive feature.

Reason 2: Understand a changing profession

The dental workforce is changing, including more affiliation with dental support organizations. If you hope to open a practice one day, or if you never want to own a business, you deserve an accurate picture of what these changes mean and what the research does and does not show.

My argument is simple: dental students should evaluate DSOs and independent practices as different sets of opportunities and responsibilities, not as a contest with one universal winner. The best choice will depend on clinical goals, career stage, financial situation, and the specific employment agreement. The point of researching this topic is to make those tradeoffs visible.

First, the vocabulary

DSO does not automatically mean private equity.

DSO

Dental Support Organization

A company or organization that supports nonclinical aspects of dental practices, which can include scheduling, billing, staffing support, human resources, technology, purchasing, and marketing. The arrangement varies by organization and state law.

Independent practice

Dentist-led ownership

A practice owned independently by a dentist or dentists, where the owners generally take on decisions and risks associated with operating a small business. An independent office can still employ associate dentists and purchase outside management services.

Private equity

An investment model

Investment firms that buy interests in businesses in hopes of increasing their value. Private equity may invest in dental service businesses or affiliated organizations, but not every DSO is private-equity backed and not every large group is a DSO.

Why this distinction matters: Two statistics that look similar may describe different things. A study of private-equity ownership cannot be used as proof of the exact percentage of dentists who work with DSOs, and a large group practice is not automatically corporate-owned.
Research point 01 · Growth

The numbers show that affiliation is growing.

According to the American Dental Association’s Health Policy Institute, the share of U.S. dentists affiliated with DSOs rose from 7.2% in 2015 to 16.1% in 2024. That is more than double the earlier share. The same report shows that in 2024, 26.5% of dentists who were ten years or less out of dental school were DSO-affiliated, compared with 9.1% of those more than 25 years out. This is especially relevant to students approaching graduation. [1]

In a separate peer-reviewed study published in Health Affairs, Kamyar Nasseh and colleagues found that private-equity affiliation nearly doubled between 2015 and 2021. Affiliation increased especially among larger dental practices and some specialties. The authors also found that private-equity-affiliated practices were more likely to participate in Medicaid, but they did not conclude that affiliation improved patient care or affordability. [2]

The growth of these business arrangements makes the conversation necessary. It does not prove that independent practices are disappearing or that dentists are all making the same career choice. A national trend describes the industry; your contract and the office where you work will determine much of your day-to-day experience.

Research point 02 · Practice models

Two paths, different tradeoffs.

There is no standard DSO employment experience and no single type of private practice. These comparisons are questions to investigate with prospective employers, not guarantees about every setting.

DSO-affiliated setting

What may appeal to you

  • Administrative assistance that can reduce the time spent on billing, staffing, purchasing, or marketing.
  • Access to shared technology, centralized systems, and potentially established workflows.
  • A chance to begin as an associate without immediately financing an office purchase.
  • Possible formal mentorship, training, or a network of colleagues, depending on the organization.

Questions worth asking

Who makes clinical decisions? How are production targets set? Can you choose laboratories or materials? What happens if you want to leave? Mentorship and benefits should be written into the offer, not merely implied.

Independent setting

What may appeal to you

  • Potentially closer involvement with an owner dentist and the decisions that shape a practice.
  • Exposure to the personal relationships and business choices involved in running one office.
  • For owners, more direct control over hiring, investments, scheduling, and practice identity.
  • A possible route into partnership or ownership, when an actual agreement provides one.

Questions worth asking

Are training and benefits available? What is the owner’s management style? Is there a clear path to buy in? Who handles compliance and administration? Independence brings opportunities, but also financial and operational responsibilities.

Keep the comparison fair: Working as an associate in an independently owned practice is not the same thing as owning a practice. Ownership means assuming additional costs, legal duties, and risk. An associate in either setting should still examine the specific contract.
Research point 03 · Work and autonomy

Job satisfaction is more complicated than a paycheck.

In a 2015 study in The Journal of the American Dental Association, Lo Sasso and colleagues analyzed responses from 2,171 dentists working in solo, small-group, and large-group settings. Dentists in small groups reported the highest overall satisfaction, while those in large groups reported greater satisfaction with income and benefits than solo practitioners and the least stress. The study compared practice size, not a clean division between DSOs and independent offices. It also identified associations, not proof that the setting itself caused the differences. [3]

For a dental student, the lesson is to define what satisfaction means personally. Someone who values predictable hours might assess a job differently from someone who wants a pathway to ownership. A practice with impressive equipment can still be a poor fit if the mentorship is weak or the treatment schedule is unrealistic. Similarly, a small office might offer close professional guidance but have fewer formal benefits.

A separate ADA study of early-career dentists found a general preference for unaffiliated private practice. It also reported that only about 10% to 15% of new dentists in group practices transitioned to solo practice over a five-year period. This is a useful reality check on the idea that every dentist who starts at a DSO will automatically become a practice owner in a few years. Some may, but an intention is different from an observed career transition. [4]

Do not ask only, “Which setting pays more?” Ask, “Which setting will let me become the dentist I want to be?”

Student debt belongs in the conversation, but it should not be treated as a complete explanation. The ADA’s early-career study found that education debt levels did not vary substantially by practice setting among the new dentists it studied. Debt can matter greatly to an individual, even if the research does not show it explains the overall difference in practice choice. [4]

Research point 04 · Ownership and patients

Can independence and corporate support coexist?

Dental students sometimes hear the career decision presented as a permanent split: either become a corporate dentist or open your own office. Real careers can be less linear. The ADA’s 2025 research on practice ownership suggests that younger dentists are still becoming owners, but many are taking that step later than previous generations. That does not mean ownership is guaranteed or that DSO employment necessarily prepares someone to buy a practice. It means the timing of ownership is an important part of the story. [5]

In my classroom discussion, two students said they would prefer to begin at a larger organization before moving into independent practice. Their reasoning centered on stability, experience, and not immediately handling an entire business. Their answers changed how I thought about the debate. Rather than treating DSOs and independent practices only as rivals, I began considering whether they could represent different stages of a dentist’s career. Those two responses are useful perspectives, not evidence that all dental students feel the same way.

There is also a larger responsibility beyond employment: patients depend on dentists to make treatment decisions based on clinical need. Corporate involvement could provide resources for technology or administrative efficiency, but financial incentives and professional autonomy deserve scrutiny in all business models. Nasseh and colleagues specifically call for more research on the effects of private equity in dentistry on care affordability and quality. Their study does not establish whether the care patients receive is better or worse. [2]

That uncertainty is not a reason to ignore the issue. It is a reason to ask precise questions. Who supervises treatment decisions? How are productivity expectations communicated? How do practices handle unnecessary-care concerns? What policies protect patients when business goals and clinical judgment may conflict? These are practical topics for interviews and due diligence, not assumptions about any particular employer.

For future owners, independent practice will still require learning skills that are rarely the center of clinical education: cash flow, insurance reimbursement, staffing, compliance, and operational planning. My reading of a practice-management chapter about a physical therapy business illustrated those pressures. Because it was not dental-specific research, I use it as background for thinking about business responsibilities, rather than as proof that one dental ownership model succeeds more often than another.

Put the research to work

A three-step checklist for your first job search.

Set your priorities

Rank what you actually value: clinical mentorship, patient relationships, predictable hours, compensation, benefits, location, specialty opportunities, and possible ownership. Your priorities can change; writing them down makes an offer easier to evaluate.

Ask for specifics

Request written details on base pay and production formulas, insurance and retirement benefits, scheduling, continuing education, restrictive covenants, lab policies, termination terms, and who controls clinical decisions. Ask to meet dentists currently working there.

Compare evidence and lived experience

Read the agreement, visit the practice, ask about turnover and mentorship, and consult a qualified professional before signing important legal or financial terms. Use national statistics for context, not as a substitute for investigating the specific employer.

Questions to take into an interview: Who provides mentorship and how often? Who decides appointment length and treatment plans? How is compensation calculated? What is the turnover rate? Is there a documented path to leadership or ownership? What happens if I decide to leave?

There may not be a perfect first job. A more realistic goal is a setting where you can grow clinically, treat patients responsibly, and understand the professional and financial obligations you accept. For a new dentist, having options is valuable. Knowing how to evaluate those options is even better.
